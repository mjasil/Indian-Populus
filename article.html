Threads of Power: how the handloom became India's loudest statement

From the looms of Varanasi to the front rows of Milan, a new generation is turning six yards of woven cotton into an argument about who gets to define modern India.

AP
Ananya Pillai
Editor-at-Large
4 June 2026 · 14 min read · 47 views
Cover image — handloom weaver, Varanasi

A weaver at work in Varanasi's Benaras district. Photo: Indian Populus

The sari has always been political. Long before it became a symbol of cultural pride or a diplomatic prop, it was a tool of economic resistance — Mahatma Gandhi understood this when he encouraged Indians to spin their own cloth and reject British imports. What is new, in 2026, is that this resistance has moved from the village square to the fashion week runway.

A quiet revolution is happening in Indian textiles. Weavers who once struggled to survive are now finding themselves at the centre of a global conversation about slow fashion, cultural identity, and the ethics of production. The question being asked — quietly, insistently — is: who benefits when Indian craft becomes international trend?

"We don't weave fabric. We weave memory. Every motif has a name, a history, a community that keeps it alive."

— Ramji Prasad, master weaver, Varanasi

The Loom as a Political Object

In the bylanes of Varanasi's Benaras district, Ramji Prasad has been weaving silk for forty-three years. His loom is a family heirloom — wooden, creaking, extraordinarily precise. He learned from his father, who learned from his. The motifs he reproduces — the dancing peacock, the flowering vine, the geometric border — are centuries old.

But something has shifted in recent years. Buyers who used to negotiate aggressively — pushing prices down to levels that made the craft barely viable — are now paying more. Some are paying significantly more. The reason, Prasad says with quiet satisfaction, is that people have begun to understand what they are buying.

From Village to Vogue

In September 2025, a Varanasi silk sari appeared on the cover of an Italian fashion magazine. The model wearing it was Indian. The photographer was Indian. The caption noted, unusually, the name of the weaver who made it.

This is new. For decades, Indian craft entered global fashion anonymously — as "inspiration", as "print", as aesthetic absorbed without attribution. The handloom weave became a pattern. The block print became a wallpaper. The artisan became invisible.

What has changed is a combination of factors: a growing Indian middle class with disposable income and cultural pride, a global slow-fashion movement that has made provenance commercially valuable, and a new generation of Indian designers who have decided that attribution is not optional.

The Numbers Behind the Story

India's handloom sector employs approximately 35 lakh weavers — the second-largest source of employment in the country after agriculture. Yet for decades, the average weaver earned less than a construction labourer. The economics were brutal: raw material costs were rising, middlemen were extracting margins at every stage, and synthetic fabrics were undercutting handloom on price.

The shift began, ironically, with the pandemic. When global supply chains collapsed, buyers who had previously imported cheap fabric from mills in Bangladesh or China began looking locally. They found, to their surprise, that Indian handloom could compete — not on price, but on story, on quality, on the kind of differentiation that consumers in saturated markets are willing to pay for.

Who Benefits?

The question that hovers over all of this is the oldest one in the history of craft and commerce: when traditional knowledge becomes fashionable, who captures the value?

The optimistic answer is that direct-to-weaver models — enabled by e-commerce and by designers who insist on transparent supply chains — are shifting the balance. Some weavers are now earning three to four times what they made a decade ago.

The pessimistic answer is that fashion is fickle. The sari that appears on an Italian magazine cover this season will be forgotten next season. And when the trend moves on, the weavers who scaled up production — who hired additional workers, who invested in more looms — will be left with inventory they cannot sell.
